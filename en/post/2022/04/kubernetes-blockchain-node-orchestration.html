Orchestrating blockchain nodes on Kubernetes at Blockdaemon: the design case

Published on
Sat, Apr 9, 2022
blockchain
kubernetes
platform-engineering
infrastructure-as-code

Stateless assumptions don't survive contact with chain data

Kubernetes starts from a familiar assumption: a workload can disappear and another instance can take its place. The database holds the state, while the process is interchangeable. Restart a pod and the user should never notice.

Blockchain nodes are the extreme opposite. Running them on Kubernetes is in the same category as running databases on Kubernetes. The industry has debated that one for years without reaching a universal answer (because there isn't one). Scale, team, and tolerance for operational complexity decide whether the trade makes sense. A Bitcoin full node carries several hundred gigabytes of chain history. An Ethereum archive node exceeds 4 TiB and keeps growing as blocks are added. The chain data is the node; the process merely reads and advances it. Restart the process and it picks up from disk (which is fine). Delete the disk and you're resyncing from genesis, which on a busy mainnet can take weeks.

That's the first mismatch. Kubernetes was designed around ephemeral, stateless workloads, and blockchain nodes are some of the most stateful workloads in existence. Adding an orchestration layer on top of that doesn't make the statefulness go away (it just adds more abstraction between you and the disk).

This post started as a design proposal at Blockdaemon in Q2/2022. It explores whether Kubernetes could serve as the orchestration layer for blockchain nodes at scale, what it would take to make that work, and where the proposal still leaves open questions.

One node, one signer, non-negotiable

Full nodes sync and serve chain data for JSON-RPC calls. They can run multiple replicas without issue. Validators cannot.

A validator's job is to sign blocks on behalf of a staker. In proof-of-stake networks, signing the same block twice (from two concurrently running validator instances) triggers slashing: an on-chain penalty that permanently destroys part of the staked funds. There is no remediation. The standard Kubernetes answer to high availability (run N replicas, let the scheduler handle restarts) is actively dangerous for validators.

This is the singleton problem: exactly one validator process may run at any given moment. The usual orchestration primitives for availability and zero-downtime upgrades need deliberate adaptation before they can enforce that rule. Get it wrong with a web service and you cause a temporary error spike. Get it wrong with a validator and you can cause permanent financial loss.

Q: Isn't that risk overstated? Surely Kubernetes won't start two replicas of a singleton at the same time.

A: It can (and does) during rolling updates. The default strategy starts the new pod before terminating the old one. Without explicit configuration, two signing processes can run simultaneously. The useful question is not whether Kubernetes is generally safe for stateful workloads. It is whether the default behavior is safe for a validator. It is not.

The case for orchestration (and the case against it)

If you're running two or three validator nodes, dedicated VMs and manual runbooks are probably the right answer. The operational overhead is bounded, the tooling is minimal, and the failure modes are well-understood. You don't need Kubernetes to manage three nodes. You need a good runbook and someone who reads it.

At scale (dozens of protocols and hundreds of nodes across multiple environments), the argument shifts. Every protocol adds more runbooks, more on-call burden, and more failure modes waiting to be discovered in production. Fleet upgrades across unorchestrated nodes mean either rolling manual execution (slow and error-prone) or custom automation for every protocol (expensive to build and maintain).

Then the counter-argument becomes real: Kubernetes adds layers. Every layer can fail, needs an owner who understands it, and interacts with the layers below in ways that are not always obvious. A platform engineering team that knows Kubernetes well may not know blockchain node operations. A team that knows blockchain operations may not know Kubernetes internals. The overlap between those skill sets is narrow (and hiring for it is expensive).

The honest framing is that orchestration could replace manual per-node toil with platform-level complexity. Whether that trade is favorable depends on the fleet, the team, and whether the platform can be built once and maintained cheaply (or becomes another system demanding constant attention). There is no universal answer.

The problem nobody talks about until the PVC fills up

Storage is where most Kubernetes-for-blockchain designs encounter their first serious wall. The standard PVC model assumes storage is relatively small, fungible, and network-attached. Chain data is none of those things.

The constraints compound:

  • Size grows without bound. Chain data ranges from tens of gigabytes for newer protocols to multiple terabytes for archive nodes, then keeps adding blocks indefinitely.
  • Formats are protocol-specific. Some chains write data in formats built around local disk access patterns. Moving that data to another node may require a full resync.
  • Sync time becomes recovery time. If storage fails and cannot be recovered, resyncing from genesis can take days or weeks. Storage reliability matters in a way it does not for a typical web workload.
  • I/O performance matters. Block validation and state transitions are I/O intensive. Network-attached storage latency (acceptable for most applications) can measurably affect node performance on high-throughput chains.

Three storage approaches worth considering

OpenEBS with Mayastor

OpenEBS implements Container Attached Storage (CAS): an abstraction layer between Kubernetes' Container Storage Interface and the underlying driver (EBS, NFS, local disk, or otherwise). The premise is that Kubernetes should orchestrate storage the same way it orchestrates compute: as containers with scheduling, affinity rules, and auto-scaling.

Mayastor is OpenEBS's distributed block storage engine, responsible for orchestrating disk placement across nodes. The design mirrors the Kubernetes control plane. Mayastor runs as containers and manages data node distribution to minimize latency. That would let storage capacity scale independently from the workload pods that mount it.

Potential operational benefits for blockchain workloads:

  • No cloud vendor lock-in. The same storage class could work across AWS, GCP, bare metal, or any combination of them.
  • Storage auto-scaling decoupled from node auto-scaling. Disk capacity could grow without resizing or restarting the workload.
  • One consistent interface, regardless of the underlying driver.

Known limitation (as of Q2/2022): no native snapshot support. Velero provides a partial workaround but not a complete solution. Snapshots matter for blockchain nodes because they enable bootstrapping new nodes from a recent chain state rather than syncing from genesis.

cStor

cStor is OpenEBS's ZFS-backed storage engine. Where Mayastor focuses on distributed block storage, cStor focuses on consistency and data management capabilities.

cStor implements copy-on-write semantics, RAID replication across nodes, PersistentVolume snapshots and cloning, and ZFS deduplication. Deduplication is particularly relevant for blockchain workloads: when multiple nodes in the same cluster hold structurally similar chain data (e.g. full nodes for the same protocol), actual disk usage could fall significantly.

The trade-off is operational: cStor requires ZFS installed on every Kubernetes node. Adding or replacing cluster nodes requires ZFS provisioning as part of the process, adding friction compared to Mayastor's more self-contained model.

ZFS nodes with NFS provisioner

This is the longer road, but also the most proven: a set of ZFS-backed nodes provisioned across availability zones and sharing volumes with the Kubernetes cluster through the NFS subdir provisioner. More moving parts, more manual configuration (and fewer unknowns in production). When the more automated options encounter edge cases, this approach falls back to primitives that have been running in production for decades.

Q: Why not just use cloud-managed block storage (EBS, Persistent Disk, etc.)?

A: Cloud-managed block storage works and is the path of least resistance at small scale. Latency characteristics are acceptable for most workloads, though they may not be for high-throughput validation. More practically: at scale across multiple cloud providers, storage vendor lock-in becomes a real operational constraint. A storage abstraction that works across providers is worth the added complexity if multi-cloud operation is the goal (but only if the team can actually maintain that abstraction).

ⓘ

The storage landscape for Kubernetes has continued to evolve since this design was written. Longhorn has since matured as a further alternative worth evaluating (particularly for its native snapshot support and simpler operational model compared to Mayastor and cStor).

The one rule Kubernetes wasn't designed for

Kubernetes rolling updates start new pods before terminating the old ones. For stateless services, this is ideal: zero downtime and no dropped requests. For validators, it means two signing processes running simultaneously (double-signing territory).

Two mitigations could compose to close this window.

Pod Disruption Budgets constrain how many pods Kubernetes may terminate at once. Setting maxUnavailable: 0 prevents termination until a replacement is ready. The complementary maxSurge: 0 setting prevents the new pod from starting before the old one is fully terminated. Together, these could enforce a stop-then-start sequence for the validator (no overlap and no double-signing window during planned upgrades).

Blocks-behind self-termination addresses the unplanned case. A liveness probe compares the validator's block height with the current network tip. If the lag exceeds a protocol-specific threshold, the probe fails and Kubernetes terminates the pod. The logic is simple: a validator significantly behind the network tip is not signing correctly anyway, so self-termination and restart are safer than letting it continue. This is sometimes called the harakiri pattern: the process detects an invalid state and exits before incorrect behavior compounds the problem.

ⓘ

The threshold would need to be protocol-specific because block times vary widely across chains. A "blocks behind" value that signals a problem on a 1-second block time chain is normal variance on a 12-second block time chain. Getting this wrong in either direction causes problems: too sensitive and the validator restarts unnecessarily; too loose and it allows a degraded validator to keep running.

Keeping keys out of the cluster

Validator keys are not like application credentials. A leaked database password gets rotated. A leaked validator private key can be used to sign blocks on behalf of the validator before the key is revoked (potentially triggering slashing between the leak and the rotation). That loss is irreversible.

Kubernetes Secrets are better than baked-in environment variables, but they are not the right primitive for keys at this sensitivity level. They are base64-encoded (not encrypted by default), accessible to any workload in the same namespace with pod-level permissions, and stored in etcd with whatever encryption posture the cluster has configured.

One approach is to separate the key store from the cluster entirely. An external secrets manager holds the key material, while an operator fetches and mounts it as a volume when the validator pod starts. The key is never at rest in the cluster. Delete the pod and the key disappears with it. Rotation does not require a redeployment: update the secret in the store and the pod picks up the new material on its next restart.

Established tools for this pattern include HashiCorp Vault (via the External Secrets Operator or the Vault Agent Injector), and cloud-native equivalents like AWS Secrets Manager or GCP Secret Manager (both of which integrate with External Secrets Operator using the same operator interface).

An active/standby validator design adds another constraint: a standby would hold no validator key at all. It connects to the peer network and syncs blocks, but cannot sign. Promoting it to active would involve writing the key to the secrets store, which the newly promoted pod mounts at startup. At any given moment, the key exists in exactly one place.

⚠

This model is conceptually clean, but its operational consequences should not be glossed over. The secrets store becomes a critical dependency: if it is unavailable at pod startup, the validator cannot start. Its availability SLA effectively becomes the validator's availability SLA. Designing for that dependency (caching, fallback, and secrets store HA) adds more surface area to maintain.

Protocol upgrades as a deployment problem

With storage, process exclusivity, and key management handled (or at least designed for), protocol upgrades could become a standard deployment problem. ArgoCD could watch for new container image tags pushed by each protocol's release pipeline and trigger rolling updates automatically. Combined with rolling update configuration that limits the batch size (e.g. 10% of the fleet at a time), plus health probes that gate each batch, a protocol upgrade could shrink from a manual per-node operation to a version tag update in a repository.

The health probe is what could make this safe. By checking blocks behind against the network reference, it provides an automated go/no-go signal. If a new protocol version introduces a regression, the rollout could stop before it reaches the full fleet (instead of monitoring discovering the problem after the fact).

Whether this fully materializes depends on how well the health probes can characterize node health for each protocol. For some chains, block lag is a complete signal. For others, a node can be synced but serve incorrect data due to state corruption (which block height alone will not catch). Protocol-specific health checks are more accurate, but the cost of building and maintaining them grows with every chain.

Shared or exclusive?

In a multi-tenant environment, node isolation becomes a design question. Two approaches lead to different trade-offs:

Kubernetes affinity and anti-affinity rules let workloads declare co-location requirements. This approach is lightweight and built into the scheduler, so it adds no runtime overhead. The resulting isolation is still container-level: processes on the same host share the kernel.

Firecracker microVMs provide hardware-level boundaries by running each workload in its own lightweight virtual machine. Firecracker (via the Containerd plugin) and Kata Containers both offer OCI-compatible runtimes that replace the standard container execution model with VM-backed isolation. The trade-off is real: microVM startup time, additional memory overhead per workload, and a more complex container runtime to configure and operate.

The threat model decides which trade-off is acceptable. Kernel-level isolation is sufficient for most operational concerns. Hardware-level isolation is warranted when a compromised container escaping to the host is in scope (a higher bar that most deployments do not require, but some do).

Is it worth it?

This is the question the design intentionally leaves open.

The argument for orchestration is scale. Manual per-node operations do not compose, and every new protocol multiplies the operational burden. Kubernetes could absorb that multiplier into one shared foundation. Rolling upgrades with health probes, automated key injection, and standardized storage interfaces are real benefits (and they compound as the fleet grows).

The argument against it is the cost of earning those benefits. Storage abstraction, singleton enforcement, external secrets integration, and protocol-specific health probes are not simple configurations. Every solution adds a layer, and every layer needs people who understand both Kubernetes primitives and blockchain-specific constraints (a rare and expensive combination). The orchestration layer could become its own operational burden, requiring more platform engineering attention than the toil it was meant to eliminate.

The break-even point (where the operational benefit exceeds the cost of building and maintaining the platform) depends on fleet size, team structure, and how much health-check and upgrade automation can be shared across protocols. At a handful of nodes, the overhead clearly is not worth it. At hundreds of nodes, it probably is. The interesting question is where that line sits in between (and whether the complexity stays bounded as the platform scales).

Open questions

  • At what fleet size does orchestration overhead become net positive?
  • How generalizable are health probes across protocols, and what's the maintenance cost of per-protocol implementations?
  • Can storage auto-scaling keep up with chain data growth rates without operator intervention?
  • What does the secrets store availability dependency cost in practice, and how is it designed for?
  • Does the team structure that can build and maintain this platform exist, or does it need to be built first?
ⓘ

The validator high availability design (the active/standby model that keeps a warm standby synced without double-signing risk) is a related problem that this proposal does not fully address.
